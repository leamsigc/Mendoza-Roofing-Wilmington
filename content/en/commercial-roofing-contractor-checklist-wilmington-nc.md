**The short answer:** compare commercial roofing contractors on six weighted criteria — commercial experience, scope and technical quality, licensing and insurance, warranty support, project management, and financial transparency. Give every bidder the same written scope, then compare lifecycle cost rather than the lowest number. Request three comparable projects and current certificates of insurance.

That sounds simple. In practice, most commercial roofing decisions go wrong before the bids arrive, because each contractor inspects the roof differently and prices a different scope. The result is three numbers that cannot be compared, and a winner chosen on price alone.

This guide is the process we use with Wilmington property owners and facilities managers, written so you can run it yourself.

## Start with a written scope, not a number

Before you call contractors, define what you are buying. A commercial roof is a system: membrane, insulation, cover board, attachment, flashing, drainage, edge metal, penetrations and code-required details. If the scope is vague, the bids will be vague.

Write down:

- The building type, size and current roof assembly.
- Whether the goal is a repair, restoration, recover, or full replacement.
- Known leaks, ponding areas, and equipment on the roof.
- Access constraints, tenant hours, and any work that cannot happen during business hours.
- Insurance, warranty, or lender requirements that affect the specification.

Then require every contractor to inspect the same roof and respond to the same scope. If a contractor proposes a different system, ask them to price your scope *and* explain the alternative separately. That keeps the comparison honest.

## The six criteria that separate commercial roofers

| Criterion | Weight | What to verify |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial experience | 25% | Three comparable projects in the last three years, same building type and roof system, with references |
| Scope and technical quality | 20% | Tear-off vs. recover, insulation R-value, cover board, membrane, flashing, drainage, edge metal, deck repairs |
| Licensing, insurance, safety | 20% | NC licensing where required, general liability, workers' comp, fall protection, hot-work plan, subcontractor coverage |
| Warranty and long-term support | 15% | Manufacturer and workmanship terms, exclusions, transferability, who handles claims, maintenance requirements |
| Project management and disruption | 10% | Schedule, phasing, maximum area opened per day, watertight procedures, named supervisor, daily cleanup |
| Financial transparency | 10% | Itemized proposal, allowances, exclusions, payment milestones, change-order rates, lien waivers |

A weighted scorecard prevents a polished sales presentation from outweighing a thin scope. Score each contractor 1–5 on every line, multiply by the weight, and total to 100. Any failure on insurance, safety, or contract terms should disqualify a bid regardless of score.

## Normalize the scope before comparing price

Two bids can differ by 30% and still both be “correct” — because they are pricing different work. Look for these common gaps:

- **Wet insulation.** One contractor includes a moisture survey and replacement; another assumes the insulation is dry.
- **Deck repairs.** Unit rates for plywood or metal deck replacement are often excluded until the roof is opened.
- **Drainage.** Ponding correction, new drains, scuppers, or tapered insulation can be a major line item.
- **Flashing and penetrations.** Pipe boots, curbs, HVAC supports, and wall terminations are where leaks start.
- **Edge metal and wind uplift.** Coastal projects need engineered attachment and edge details, not generic perimeter metal.
- **Tear-off and disposal.** Dumpsters, hauling, and landfill fees add up on large roofs.
- **Roof access and protection.** Crane time, staging, and protection for equipment or tenants.

Ask every bidder to list **inclusions, exclusions, and allowances** in the same format. Then re-price any missing critical item before you compare totals.

Use the score as a decision aid, not an automatic award. A contractor with a 78 and clean insurance, references, and a complete scope is usually a better choice than an 88 with an excluded drainage correction.

## Red flags in a commercial roofing bid

- A one-line price with no specifications or exclusions.
- No verifiable commercial references, or references that are all residential.
- A certificate of insurance that is expired, limited, or does not cover subcontractors.
- A manufacturer warranty that does not match the proposed assembly.
- A large upfront payment request, or payment due before materials arrive.
- Pressure to sign before you have compared bids.
- No explanation of how hidden deck damage, wet insulation, or weather delays will be priced.
- A schedule that ignores your operating hours, tenants, or deliveries.

The National Roofing Contractors Association recommends prequalifying contractors, checking insurance, obtaining references, and reviewing written proposals and warranties. Those four steps catch most bad outcomes.

## What Wilmington commercial roofs need that inland bids miss

Coastal New Hanover and Brunswick County roofs face wind uplift, salt-laden air, heavy rain, and humidity. A commercial proposal should address:

- **Wind uplift** — attachment pattern and edge metal designed for the building's exposure.
- **Corrosion** — fasteners, edge metal, and flashing suited to salt exposure, especially within a few miles of the ocean.
- **Drainage** — scuppers, drains, and tapered insulation that move water off the roof quickly during tropical rain.
- **Occupied buildings** — phasing, tenant access, noise, and protection for inventory or equipment.
- **Moisture** — a moisture survey before specifying a recover, so wet insulation is not sealed into the assembly.

A contractor who bids the same generic TPO assembly for a beachfront retail center and an inland warehouse is not pricing your building.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many commercial roofing bids should a business get?

Get at least three written bids when the project allows it. Give every contractor the same specifications, roof measurements, inspection findings and project requirements so the proposals are comparable. Two bids can be enough for an urgent repair, but a full replacement should have three.

### Should a business choose the lowest commercial roofing bid?

Not without normalizing the scope. A lower bid often excludes wet insulation replacement, drainage corrections, flashing details or disposal. Compare itemized scopes and total lifecycle cost, then treat any missing critical item as a reason to re-bid rather than a saving.

### What insurance should a commercial roofing contractor carry?

Ask for current general liability and workers compensation certificates, and confirm the coverage limits with the insurer. For occupied commercial buildings, also ask about subcontractor coverage, fall protection, hot-work procedures and additional-insured endorsements.
